## 1. Bandura (1977) — Self-Efficacy Theory

**Full citation:**
Bandura, A. (1977). Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change. *Psychological Review, 84*(2), 191–215.

**What the paper is about:**
Bandura introduces self-efficacy as the central mechanism explaining how psychological treatments produce behavioral change. The paper proposes a unified theoretical framework and reviews empirical evidence across multiple treatment methods.

**Key construct definition:**
Self-efficacy is defined as *the conviction that one can successfully execute the behavior required to produce a given outcome* (p. 193). Importantly, Bandura distinguishes two types of expectations:
- **Efficacy expectation** — belief that one *can* perform the behavior ("I can do this")
- **Outcome expectation** — belief that the behavior will produce a certain outcome ("doing this will work")

These are different: a person may believe that a behavior leads to good outcomes but still doubt their ability to execute it. Self-efficacy measures the first type only.

**Four sources of self-efficacy:**
1. **Performance accomplishments** — most influential; direct mastery experiences
2. **Vicarious experience** — observing others succeed
3. **Verbal persuasion** — being told one can do it (weaker, less durable)
4. **Physiological arousal** — emotional/physical state as a signal of capability

**Efficacy expectations vary in:**
- *Magnitude* — how difficult a task the person feels capable of
- *Generality* — whether it extends to similar situations
- *Strength* — how resistant it is to disconfirming experiences

**Relevance to our survey:**
- Justifies why self-efficacy items must be **forward-looking** — asking about belief in future capability, not assessment of past performance
- Our S2/S4 Q1 ("I feel confident in my ability to negotiate effectively") and Q2 ("I believe I could handle a similar negotiation successfully") directly operationalize efficacy expectations in the negotiation domain
- The S2→S4 delta measures whether the intervention (debrief / reflection / control) changed participants' efficacy expectations — the core self-report DV

## 2. Grant, Franklin & Langford (2002) — Self-Reflection and Insight Scale (SRIS)

**Full citation:**
Grant, A. M., Franklin, J., & Langford, P. (2002). The self-reflection and insight scale: A new measure of private self-consciousness. *Social Behavior and Personality, 30*(8), 821–836.

**What the paper is about:**
Grant et al. develop and validate the SRIS, a new measure of private self-consciousness designed to capture metacognitive processes relevant to behavior change. It improves on the earlier Private Self-Consciousness Scale (PrSCS) by distinguishing two conceptually separate factors.

**Key construct definitions:**
- **Self-reflection (SRIS-SR)** — "the inspection and evaluation of one's thoughts, feelings and behavior" (p. 821)
- **Insight (SRIS-IN)** — "the clarity of understanding of one's thoughts, feelings and behavior" (p. 821)

Both are described as *metacognitive factors central to the process of purposeful, directed behavior change*. The paper situates them within a self-regulation cycle: self-reflection enables monitoring of performance; insight enables evaluation and corrective action.

**Key finding:** Self-reflection and insight are *logically independent* — a person can spend considerable time in self-reflection without gaining insight. This distinction matters for our study: the debrief is designed to produce both.

**Scale structure (from Table 1, factor loadings):**
- SRIS-SR (α = .91): items about frequency of examining feelings, taking time to reflect, thinking about behavior — engagement in the reflective process
- SRIS-IN (α = .87): items about having a clear idea of why one behaved a certain way, knowing why one feels the way one does — clarity of self-understanding

**Relevance to our survey:**
- Justifies the insight subscale in S2 and S4, specifically the SRIS-IN factor
- We focus only on **insight** (not self-reflection), because what matters is the *outcome* of the reflective process — did the participant gain genuine understanding? Self-reflection is just the process to get there.
- All three insight items (Q3–Q5 in S2/S4) use "understand" or "clear understanding" as the core framing, consistent with SRIS-IN item wording
- S1 Q2–Q4 are treated as simple baseline self-knowledge items — they do not use the SRIS framework
- The S2→S4 insight delta tests whether the intervention (debrief / reflection / none) produced greater self-understanding — the primary mechanism the Sage debrief is designed to activate

## 3. Rigotti, Schyns & Mohr (2008) — Occupational Self-Efficacy Scale (OSS-6)

**Full citation:**
Rigotti, T., Schyns, B., & Mohr, G. (2008). A short version of the occupational self-efficacy scale: Structural and construct validity across five countries. *Journal of Career Assessment, 16*(2), 238–255.

**What the paper is about:**
Rigotti et al. validate a 6-item short version of the Occupational Self-Efficacy Scale (OSS-6) across five countries (Germany, Sweden, Belgium, Great Britain, Spain; N=1,535). The paper tests structural, metric, and scalar equivalence across samples.

**Key construct definition:**
Occupational self-efficacy = *the competence a person feels concerning the ability to successfully fulfill the tasks involved in their job* — a domain-specific measure grounded in Bandura (1977). It sits between general self-efficacy (too broad) and task-specific self-efficacy (too narrow) — making it suitable for evaluating training interventions in a defined skill domain.

**The 6 OSS items (English/British version, from Appendix):**
1. I can remain calm when facing difficulties in my job because I can rely on my abilities.
2. When I am confronted with a problem in my job, I can usually find several solutions.
3. Whatever comes my way in my job, I can usually handle it.
4. My past experiences in my job have prepared me well for my occupational future.
5. I meet the goals that I set for myself in my job.
6. I feel prepared for most of the demands in my job.

**Psychometric properties:**
- Cronbach's α = .85–.90 across all five countries
- CFA goodness-of-fit GFI > .90 for all country samples
- Positively correlated with job satisfaction, commitment, and performance; negatively correlated with job insecurity — supporting construct validity

**Relevance to our survey:**
- Provides validated item framing for our S2/S4 self-efficacy subscale (Q1–Q2)
- Our items are adapted to the negotiation domain, following the same forward-looking, capability-focused structure as the OSS-6
- Notably, OSS item 6 ("I feel prepared for most of the demands in my job") is close to our earlier draft wording — the revision to "I believe I could handle a similar negotiation successfully" better captures Bandura's efficacy expectation framing
- The OSS-6 uses a 6-point scale; our study uses 7-point Likert — a minor adaptation consistent with HCI survey norms

## 4. Duddu et al. (2025) — Does AI Coaching Prepare Us for Workplace Negotiations?

**Full citation:**
Duddu, V., Parekh, J. R., Mao, A., Min, H., Xiao, Z., Saha, K., & Das Swain, V. (2025). Does AI coaching prepare us for workplace negotiations? *arXiv preprint arXiv:2509.22545.*

**What the paper is about:**
Duddu et al. build and evaluate Trucey, an AI negotiation coach grounded in Brett & Thompson's negotiation framework. They run a pre-registered between-subjects experiment (N=267) comparing three conditions: Trucey (AI + theory-driven), ChatGPT (AI only), and Handbook (static theory, no AI). The study focuses on psychological readiness for negotiation — targeting self-efficacy and fear reduction rather than just tactical skill.

**Study design:**
- N = 267, randomly assigned ~2:1:1 (Trucey:ChatGPT:Handbook)
- Scenarios: requesting a raise, promotion, or time off
- Pre and post-task surveys for all participants
- Followed by semi-structured interviews (N=15)

**Survey measures used:**
- **OSS-6** (Rigotti et al., 2008) — occupational self-efficacy, pre and post ✓
- **PEU** (Personal Empowerment Understanding) — 4 dimensions: meaning, competence, self-determination, impact
- **Negotiation preparedness** — fear (2 custom items) + willingness to initiate (2 custom items), 5-point Likert
- **UMUX** (usability, post only)
- **IAM** (intervention appropriateness, post only)

**Key findings:**
- Handbook outperformed both AI conditions on usability and psychological empowerment
- Trucey significantly reduced negotiation fear more than ChatGPT (Cohen's *d* = −0.23)
- Participants valued Trucey's rehearsal component but found AI guidance fragmented and insufficient for cross-scenario preparation
- Theory-driven content (Handbook) produced more confidence and preparedness than conversational AI alone

**Relevance to our survey:**
- Most direct precedent study: AI-assisted negotiation training with pre/post self-efficacy measurement using OSS-6
- Confirms that OSS-6 is appropriate for negotiation self-efficacy in training research
- The finding that psychological readiness (efficacy, fear) matters independently of tactical skill directly motivates our decision to measure both self-efficacy and metacognitive awareness — not just behavioral outcomes
- Unlike Duddu et al., our study adds a debrief condition and measures metacognitive awareness (SRIS-based), which is absent from their design

## How These Four Papers Connect to the Survey Instrument

| Paper | What it provides |
|---|---|
| Bandura (1977) | Theoretical definition of self-efficacy — why items must be forward-looking; distinguishes efficacy from outcome expectations |
| Grant et al. (2002) | Theoretical definition and scale structure for metacognitive awareness (self-reflection + insight) — justifies Q3–Q5 in S2/S4 |
| Rigotti et al. (2008) | Validated item framing and psychometric benchmarks for domain-specific self-efficacy — justifies Q1–Q2 in S2/S4 |
| Duddu et al. (2025) | Closest precedent study — confirms OSS-6 use in AI negotiation training, motivates psychological readiness as a key outcome |
